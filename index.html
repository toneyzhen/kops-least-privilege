Permission Blast Radius

An attacker broke into one app. Switch its permissions off and watch how far they can reach shrink. What do these words mean?

Try switching off the permissions the app doesn't need. Select a dot in the diagram to see how the attacker gets there.

Why this matters

  1. Unneeded permissions add risk and give nothing back.
  2. Permissions chain together: one small one can open bigger ones.
  3. Remove what the job doesn't need, then block the dangerous paths.
The details, and how kOps handles each
  • Nothing extra. The app only reads files from its own folder. Every other switch makes the blast radius bigger and does not help the job. In kOps setups, IRSA gives each app its own small AWS key card instead of sharing the server's.
  • Chains. Reading every bucket exposes the cluster's master keys, and the master keys give full control of the cluster. This project limits the app to one folder, so the chain never starts.
  • Block the paths. Closing the shortcuts to the server's keys and to other apps keeps a break-in small. kOps setups set the metadata hop limit to 1, add a default-deny NetworkPolicy, and write scoped RBAC roles with no secrets or pods/exec.

What do these words mean?
Blast radius
How much damage one break-in can cause. Fewer permissions mean a smaller radius.
Least privilege
Giving a person or program only the access its job needs, and nothing more.
AWS
Amazon's cloud, where you rent computers, storage, and more.
IAM
AWS's key-card system: it decides who may do what in an AWS account.
Kubernetes
Software that runs many small apps across a group of computers and keeps them healthy.
kOps
A tool that builds and maintains a Kubernetes cluster on AWS.
Pod
One running copy of an app inside the cluster.
S3 bucket
A folder in the cloud for storing files.
Certificate authority (CA) key
The master key that proves who is who inside the cluster. Whoever holds it can forge an admin login.
Instance metadata (IMDS)
A special address a server can ask for its own AWS keys. If an attacker reaches it, they can borrow the server's identity.
NetworkPolicy
A Kubernetes rule that says which apps may talk to which. A default-deny policy blocks everything that is not listed.
RBAC
Kubernetes's key-card rules: which person or app may do which action on which thing inside the cluster.
IRSA
A way to give one app its own small AWS key card, instead of sharing the whole server's.
Secret
Kubernetes's safe for passwords and tokens.
